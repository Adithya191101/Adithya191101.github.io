Project Overview

During the Christmas and New Year break of 2025, I set out to explore how Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models and transformer-based policies work on real robots. What started as curiosity turned into a four-month research project that pushed a 500M parameter model from 0% to 85% success rate on a real manipulation task, through systematic debugging, curriculum training, and one critical inference discovery.

The setup: a SO-101 6-DOF manipulator arm with Feetech STS3215 servos, a low-quality Logitech 720p USB webcam as the front camera, and a basic camera from the SO-101 kit mounted at the wrist. SmolVLA supports three camera inputs, but I only had two. Both cameras run at 640x480 with noticeable noise and limited dynamic range. Not ideal, but that's what makes the results meaningful.

The task: grasp a deformable brain toy and place it inside a green container. This isn't a standard pick-and-place. The brain toy is squeezable (deforms under gripper pressure), has irregular geometry (no consistent grasp surface), and is elastic (bounces away on imprecise contact). The robot needs to approach from the right angle, apply the right pressure, and not drop the toy during transport.

I collected 239 teleoperation episodes (99,845 frames at 30 FPS) using the SO-101 leader arm, trained two different architectures (ACT and SmolVLA), and resolved 17 distinct technical challenges across two phases of development.

Phase 1: Exploration and Diagnosis (December 2025 - January 2026)

ACT: The Baseline That Worked

I started with the Action Chunking Transformer (ACT) [3] as a baseline. ACT uses a ResNet18 CNN encoder, a Transformer backbone, and a CVAE decoder to predict action chunks of 100 steps. It trains all ~20M parameters end-to-end, including the vision backbone. After 50K steps on a V100 GPU (~6 hours), ACT achieved 80% success rate. The robot produced smooth, coordinated trajectories with temporal ensembling. A solid starting point.

SmolVLA: The Ambitious Attempt

Next, I moved to SmolVLA [1] — a 500M parameter Vision-Language-Action model built on SmolVLM2-500M-Video-Instruct. Unlike ACT, SmolVLA understands language instructions. Its architecture: a frozen SigLIP vision encoder (pretrained on internet-scale images) feeds into a language model expert, which generates actions through Conditional Flow Matching [5]. Only the action expert head (~100M parameters) is trainable.

I trained 9 versions (v1-v9) exploring every dimension: chunk sizes (10, 15, 30, 50), learning rates (1e-4, 4.24e-5), weight decay, augmentation strategies. I ran 30 Optuna HPO trials (15 completed, 15 pruned). The best trial (#11) found lr=4.24e-5, weight_decay=8.49e-5, chunk_size=10. Loss looked great.

But on the robot: maximum 33% success across all 9 versions. Every model showed the same behavior — the robot approaches the object correctly (visual understanding works), but the gripper oscillates wildly between open and closed near the grasp point. Max step-to-step gripper change: 30.1 degrees. The robot couldn't commit to a grasp.

Diagnosing the Gripper Oscillation

I added action logging to capture every prediction during inference. The data told the story: 76% of training frames have the gripper open (approach phase), only 12% show active grasping. With MEAN_STD normalization, predicting a grasp requires the model to output a +2.21 sigma prediction — a rare, high-confidence event that the model hedges against by oscillating between "keep approaching" and "grasp now."

All 9 versions showed identical behavior despite wildly different hyperparameters. My initial conclusion: SmolVLA 500M lacks the capacity for precise manipulation. Move to Pi0.5 (3B parameters).

That conclusion was wrong. The model was fine. The training methodology was wrong.

Phase 1: Seven Debugging Challenges

Before reaching the 33% mark, I resolved seven critical deployment failures through systematic investigation. Each taught a fundamental lesson about VLA deployment.

1. Language Instruction Mismatch (0% success)
The robot had 0% success despite 100% success in training data. Root cause: training used "Grab the brain" but deployment used "Grasp a brain and put it in the bin." VLA models create entirely different embeddings for semantically similar but textually different instructions. Using the exact training string fixed success from 0% to 33%. This took two weeks to discover — the first week was spent suspecting overfitting.
2. Camera Configuration Swap (Complete failure)
After adjusting the environment, the robot started crashing into the bin instead of approaching the object. All models failed identically. The cameras had been physically swapped: the front camera was wired to the wrist input and vice versa. The model's spatial reasoning was completely inverted. Running --display_data=true to visually verify camera feeds revealed the swap instantly. Lesson: always visually verify camera assignments before deployment.
3. Overfitting (15.87 epochs on 241 episodes)
Training for 50,000 steps with batch=32 resulted in 15.87 epochs — far too many for a 241-episode dataset (industry standard: 3-7 epochs for this size). The model memorized specific trajectories. Reduced to 20,000 steps (6.35 epochs), cutting training from 9 hours to 3.5 hours with better generalization.
4. Action Smoothness vs Reactivity
chunk_size=10 produced jerky motion (10-26 degree jumps between frames). chunk_size=30 was smoother but less reactive. This fundamental trade-off — trajectory smoothness versus visual feedback frequency — would later be resolved in Phase 2 by decoupling chunk_size from n_action_steps.
5. Robot Calibration Drift
Joint offsets drift over time with Feetech servos. Must recalibrate before every evaluation session.
6. Starting State Inconsistency
Success varied 0-33% between episodes because the robot didn't auto-reset. Training data started with gripper closed at 0.5 degrees; deployment started with gripper open at 2.8-40 degrees. Implementing a consistent home position reset (especially gripper CLOSED) before each episode improved consistency.
7. Visual Distribution Shift
Camera physically moved since data collection. Built a live preview tool to compare current camera view against training data screenshots from the HuggingFace dataset visualizer.

The Turning Point: Why Curriculum Training?

After Phase 1, I stepped back and studied the literature more carefully. Three insights changed the direction of the project:

Insight 1: The SmolVLA paper ablation tables [1] showed that lr=1e-5 gives 0% success rate. Our Optuna HPO had converged toward lower learning rates — exactly the wrong direction. The paper's recommended config (batch=64, lr=1e-4, frozen vision, expert-only training) was fundamentally different from what HPO found optimal on our loss landscape.

Insight 2: Diffusion Policy [2] demonstrated that behavior policies can be trained through iterative denoising — starting from random noise and progressively refining into precise actions. SmolVLA uses Conditional Flow Matching [5], which follows the same principle at inference: iteratively denoise a noise sample into actions through multiple denoising steps.

Insight 3: If iterative refinement works for converting noise into actions at inference time, why not apply it at training time too? Start with a generic pretrained policy and progressively refine it into a specialized one through multiple training iterations. This is curriculum learning [7] applied to policy fine-tuning.

This approach was particularly suited to our constraints:

  • Small dataset (239 episodes): Training from scratch doesn't provide enough signal. Starting from pretrained weights preserves the VLM's prior knowledge of objects and spatial relationships.
  • Unique deformable object: The brain toy has no standard grasp geometry. The model needs to discover object-specific strategies through progressive refinement, not memorize fixed trajectories.
  • Frozen vision encoder: Only ~100M of 500M parameters are trainable. Each iteration must efficiently specialize limited capacity rather than learning everything from scratch.

Phase 2: Curriculum Training to 85% (March 2026)

Step 1: LoRA Fine-Tuning (Action Space Exploration)

Applied Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA) [4] targeting q_proj/v_proj in the language model expert and state/action projections. This efficiently explored the action manifold without full parameter updates, identifying the effective action subspace for manipulating the deformable brain toy. LoRA served as a lightweight probe before committing to full training.

Step 2: Full Expert Training with Pretrained Backbone

Enabled full training of the action expert head while keeping the pretrained SmolVLM2-500M vision-language backbone frozen. This leverages the pretrained SigLIP vision encoder's internet-scale knowledge of objects, scenes, and spatial relationships, while learning task-specific action distributions through Conditional Flow Matching [5].

The training configuration that worked:

batch_size=64, lr=1e-4, weight_decay=1e-10
chunk_size=50, n_action_steps=50
freeze_vision_encoder=True, train_expert_only=True
use_amp=False
Augmentation: ColorJitter (brightness, contrast, saturation, hue),
SharpnessJitter, RandomAffine (degrees, translate)

Step 3: Three Curriculum Training Iterations

Each iteration starts from the previous checkpoint, applying curriculum learning [7] where the model progressively refines its policy. Batch size was varied across iterations following the linear scaling rule [6].

Version Batch Steps Loss Performance
Proven v1 64 50K 0.449 → 0.052 ~50% grasp, placing works consistently
Proven v2 128 20K (from v1) 0.062 → 0.038 Smoother actions, grasping improved
Proven v3 64 70K (from v2) 0.040 → 0.028 85% success (17/20+ episodes)

Batch size journey: v1 at batch=64 established the base. v2 at batch=128 gave smoother gradients and lower loss (0.038 vs 0.052). I then tried batch=200, but hit diminishing returns — the linear scaling rule [6] says larger batches need proportionally larger learning rates. I kept lr=1e-4 constant, so batch=200 was effectively under-learning. Returned to batch=64 with extended training (70K steps) for the best result: loss 0.028, the lowest achieved across the entire project.

Step 4: Inference Optimization — The Critical Breakthrough

Even after curriculum training with loss 0.028, the robot only grasped about 50% of the time. It would approach the object, try to find the perfect angle, open and close the gripper, then try a different approach — oscillating between multiple grasping strategies. The model had learned different grasps for different object positions in the training data, but couldn't commit to one at inference time.

The root cause: SmolVLA's flow matching denoising process with the default num_steps=10 produced insufficient precision. The 10-step denoising couldn't resolve the multimodal action distribution into a single, committed trajectory.

The fix: num_steps=20 (double the denoising) + n_action_steps=10 (re-observe twice as often).

This single change took success from ~50% to 85%. It was the most impactful discovery in the entire project. More denoising steps allowed the model to commit to one grasping strategy. More frequent re-observation let the model correct mid-grasp when the deformable toy shifted under pressure.

Step 5: Dataset Quality and Prompt Engineering

A comprehensive dataset audit revealed a critical data quality issue: episode 240 contained 533 frames of a completely static robot — zero action variance across all 6 joints. This garbage episode was an artifact of an earlier dataset cleaning (episode 239 was deleted, but episode 240 was orphaned in a separate parquet file). Those 533 frames of "do nothing" were silently teaching the model to hesitate during grasping.

Additionally, the evaluation prompt had drifted again: the dataset stored the task as "Grab the brain" but the client was using the full description "Grab the grey brain toy and place it inside the green container." VLA language conditioning requires exact text matching [1] — the same class of bug I had fixed in Phase 1, recurring with different text.

Evaluation Protocol and Results

All benchmarks were conducted with the policy running continuously without stopping or restarting between episodes. This is a critical methodological detail: restarting the policy resets the stochastic state of the flow matching denoising process, eliminating the natural action variability that the model relies on for adaptive behavior. Continuous execution preserves the model's exploration distribution, providing a more rigorous and realistic evaluation.

Final result: 17 successful episodes out of 20+ continuous attempts (~85% success rate).

The robot consistently grasps the deformable brain toy and places it in the target container across varied starting positions, under different lighting conditions (natural daylight, artificial overhead, dim evening), and with slight camera displacement from the original training setup. All achieved with low-quality cameras and only 2 of 3 supported camera inputs.

ACT vs SmolVLA: What We Learned

Training both architectures on the same dataset and evaluating on the same task revealed fundamental differences in how they handle real-world manipulation.

Aspect ACT [3] SmolVLA [1]
Vision ResNet18 (end-to-end trained) Frozen SigLIP (internet-scale pretrained)
Action prediction Deterministic regression Stochastic flow matching (20-step denoising)
Language understanding None Language-conditioned via SmolVLM2
Camera displacement Brittle — performance degrades Handles it without retraining
Lighting changes Degrades Consistent across conditions
Success rate 80% 85%

On deformable objects: ACT executes deterministic grasps — the same trajectory every time. For the elastic brain toy, this means it either succeeds consistently or fails consistently for a given position. SmolVLA's stochastic flow matching produces slightly different trajectories each attempt. When one approach fails (toy deforms or bounces), the next attempt naturally varies, sometimes finding a better grasp. This makes SmolVLA more resilient to the toy's unpredictable shape.

On recovery: When ACT fails a grasp, it repeats the same trajectory. SmolVLA, re-observing every 10 action steps, adjusts mid-execution. If the toy shifts during contact, the model re-plans from the new visual state.

On multi-object scaling: SmolVLA's language conditioning enables curriculum training across multiple objects without catastrophic forgetting. Train "Grab the brain" then add "Grab the cube" — different text embeddings map to different action subspaces, preserving existing skills. This is architecturally impossible with ACT.

Phase 2: Ten More Challenges

Phase 2 introduced a new set of technical challenges, from training methodology to systems engineering.

8. Training Methodology
Literature review of SmolVLA paper ablations revealed the correct config (batch=64, lr=1e-4). Our HPO had converged to lr=4.24e-5 — the paper showed lr=1e-5 gives 0% success. The issue was methodology, not model capacity.
9. Normalization Mismatch (use_degrees)
LeRobot v0.5+ changed the default normalization from RANGE_M100_100 to DEGREES. Dataset was recorded with use_degrees=False. The mismatch caused 5-degree errors on all joints. Required explicit configuration override.
10. Garbage Episode in Dataset
Episode 240: 533 frames of a static robot with zero action variance across all 6 joints. An orphan from earlier dataset cleaning. Removed from local storage and HuggingFace Hub.
11. Task Text Mismatch (Recurrence)
Same class of bug as Challenge #1, discovered again with different text. Dataset stores "Grab the brain"; eval used the full description. VLA language conditioning requires exact matching.
12. Flow Matching Denoising Precision — THE Breakthrough
Default num_steps=10 caused mode-switching between grasping strategies. Model couldn't commit. Doubling to 20 + halving n_action_steps to 10 achieved ~50% to 85% success. The single most impactful change in the entire project.
13. Batch Size and Linear Scaling Rule
Batch=200 underperformed without proportional LR increase [6]. Batch=64 with 70K steps (more gradient updates) outperformed batch=200 with the same learning rate.
14. Curriculum Checkpoint Training
Progressive refinement from checkpoints (v1→v2→v3) converged faster and to lower loss than training from scratch. Each iteration inherits and refines learned representations.
15. Remote Inference Architecture
Local RTX 3050 too slow (~2.5s per inference). Designed FastAPI server on RunPod (RTX 4090, 48GB) + multi-threaded local client with SSH tunnel. ~200-300ms round-trip latency.
16. Video Recording Thread Contention
Recording competed with inference for the robot lock, degrading control. Solved with shared frame buffer — inference writes frames, recorder reads without locking. Used pyav h264 encoder with signal handler for graceful shutdown.
17. Disk Space Management
RunPod (30GB) filling from 10 checkpoints (13GB), HF cache (8GB), wandb cache (12GB). Local machine at 1GB free. Systematic checkpoint pruning and cache cleanup.

Remote Inference Architecture

The local RTX 3050 was too slow for real-time SmolVLA inference (~2.5 seconds per action chunk). I designed a server-client split:

Dual Cameras (640x480) → Local Client (SO-101 Robot) → SSH Tunnel → RunPod GPU Server (RTX 4090) → SmolVLA (20-step denoising) → Action Chunk (50 actions) → Motor Commands @ 10 FPS

The server (smolvla_server.py) runs on RunPod as a FastAPI HTTP endpoint. It loads the model once, runs warmup inference, and serves predictions via JSON. The client (smolvla_client.py) runs locally with three threads: inference (requests action chunks), actor (executes actions on the robot), and recorder (captures video from the inference thread's shared frame buffer without contending for the robot lock). Inference latency: ~200-300ms per round-trip including network.

Key Lessons

  • Inference parameters can matter as much as training: Doubling denoising steps was the single largest improvement — bigger impact than 70K steps of additional training.
  • Curriculum training outperforms training from scratch: Progressive checkpoint refinement (v1→v2→v3) converged to lower loss faster than any single training run.
  • Pretrained vision provides environmental robustness: Frozen SigLIP handles camera displacement and lighting variation without retraining — a capability ACT's task-specific ResNet18 cannot match.
  • Language conditioning enables multi-object scaling: Different task prompts map to different action subspaces, preventing catastrophic forgetting when adding new objects.
  • Data quality has outsized impact: One garbage episode (0.5% of data) measurably degraded grasping. Dataset auditing is not optional.
  • Stochastic policies suit deformable objects: Flow matching's natural action variation helps recover from failed grasps on elastic, irregular objects.
  • HPO can converge wrong: When the search space excludes the correct configuration, optimization finds a local optimum that doesn't transfer to real performance.

Next Steps

  • Pi0.5 fine-tuning: 3B parameter VLA with LoRA [4], expected to improve precision with the same dataset
  • Multi-object curriculum training: Add cube, cylinder, and other objects to the same model via language conditioning — testing catastrophic forgetting resistance
  • Sim-to-real transfer: Leverage pretrained visual features for domain transfer from simulation
  • Multi-task VLA: Pick, place, push, stack with a single language-conditioned model
  • Open-vocabulary manipulation: Test generalization to novel language instructions not seen during training
